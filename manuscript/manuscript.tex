\title{Moment-to-Moment Affect During Film-Induced Somatic Anxiety}
\shorttitle{Moment-to-Moment Affect}
\author{Micah G. Allen}
\affiliation{Center of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience, Department of Clinical Medicine, Aarhus University,

\authornote{Micah G. Allen, ORCID 0000-0001-9399-4179.

\abstract{Watching someone in danger can make a viewer feel anxious even when they know they are safe. Studying how
this response builds, persists and subsides requires methods that capture experience as events unfold. We present an
open-source browser platform for continuous affect rating and demonstrate its use with a 13.5-minute film of unsecured
skyscraper climbing. Viewers rate valence and arousal simultaneously on an affect grid embedded in the video. The
platform records ratings alongside video and displayed-frame timestamps, pauses playback during interruptions, and
combines participant training with input logging and checks on session quality. An exploratory sample
($n$~=~\val{n_exploratory}) informed preregistered tests in an independent confirmatory sample ($N$~=~\val{n_primary}).
Arousal rose as the climb progressed, remained elevated for several minutes, and then declined, with the sustained
period experienced as most unpleasant. Group-level time courses closely reproduced across samples (arousal $r$~=~.993;
valence $r$~=~.937). Self-reported somatic anxiety increased substantially after viewing ($d_z$~=~\val{H1:est}), and
larger increases were associated with higher arousal and more unpleasant valence during the film. The platform,
analysis code and reference data are openly available. Together, these resources support online characterisation of
naturalistic stimuli and provide a shared temporal reference for studying sustained emotion, bodily experience and
their physiological and neural correlates.}

\keywords{emotion induction, continuous affect rating, affect grid, naturalistic stimuli, somatic anxiety, interoception}

Watching someone hang from the edge of a skyscraper can be uncomfortable even from the safety of a chair. As the
danger unfolds, concern for the person on screen may be accompanied by tension and other bodily sensations. Such films
offer a way to study emotion as it builds, persists and subsides. Capturing these changes requires measurements made
during the episode rather than only afterwards \parencite{saarimaki2021naturalistic}. Here we present an open-source
browser platform for continuous affect rating and use it to characterise a film of sustained vicarious danger,
examining both the reproducibility of its affective time course and changes in viewers' reported bodily anxiety.

Films are widely used for emotion induction because they present the same unfolding events to every viewer.
Established film libraries have typically been characterised using ratings collected after viewing
\parencite{gilman2017film,gross1995emotion,schaefer2010assessing}. These ratings describe the overall experience but
do not show when emotion intensifies, remains elevated or begins to recede. Continuous ratings trace experience within
the film \parencite{ruef2007continuous}, and have been related to neural responses and brain-state dynamics during
naturalistic viewing \parencite{chang2021endogenous,goldin2005neural}. The Emo-FilM dataset combines moment-to-moment
emotion annotations with physiological and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) recordings
\parencite{morgenroth2025emofilm}, while continuous anxiety ratings have been used to follow the suspense of a
Hitchcock thriller \parencite{kirk2024brainheart}. Such approaches allow researchers to examine emotional episodes
through their changing course.

Online testing makes it possible to collect these ratings from many viewers, but introduces challenges for
interpreting them. A rating must be linked to the images actually presented, and researchers need to know whether
viewing and responding proceeded as intended. Buffering or switching to another browser tab can disrupt stimulus
exposure, while periods without input may reflect either stable feelings or disengagement. Unsupervised studies must
also contend with AI-assisted and automated responding, which complicate the assessment of participation
\parencite{sehgal2026aiassisted,veselovsky2023artificial,westwood2025existential}. Recording the circumstances in
which ratings were made is therefore part of establishing what those ratings can tell us.

Existing tools support continuous annotation in browsers
\parencite{boccignone2017amhuse,farrens2020continuous,louven2022emotouch,melhart2019pagan}, on desktop computers
\parencite{girard2014carma,girard2018darma}, and in immersive virtual reality \parencite{fourcade2025affecttracker}.
Our platform brings simultaneous affect rating, controlled presentation and records of participation together in one
open workflow. Viewers move a point within an affect grid embedded in the video to report how pleasant and activating
their experience feels, building on established dimensional and continuous rating methods
\parencite{russell1980circumplex,russell1989affectgrid,schubert1999measuring}. Each rating is stored with the video
time and, where supported by the browser, the time of the displayed frame. Viewing interruptions pause the film and are
logged, while periodic saving allows sessions to resume after a page reload. Participants practise before viewing, and
input and text-entry records support subsequent checks on session quality (Online Resource 1, Fig.~S1).

We demonstrate the platform with a 13.5-min first-person film of people climbing and performing unsecured stunts on a
skyscraper. Presented without sound, the film conveys danger without requiring dialogue and builds towards a prolonged
period of exposure at height (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a). This makes it a candidate stimulus for studying sustained
unpleasant arousal and the transitions into and out of that state. To assess whether the experience also changes
reported bodily anxiety, participants completed the somatic subscale of the State-Trait Inventory for Cognitive and
Somatic Anxiety (STICSA) before and after viewing. This scale measures symptoms such as a fast heartbeat and tense
muscles \parencite{gros2007psychometric,ree2008distinguishing}. Relating these reported bodily changes to the
unfolding affective response connects the study of emotion dynamics with questions about interoceptive experience
\parencite{cristaldi2026beyond,critchley2017interoception}.

An exploratory sample ($n$~=~\val{n_exploratory}) informed preregistered hypotheses tested in an independent
confirmatory sample ($N$~=~\val{n_primary}). We predicted that arousal would rise, remain elevated and then decline;
that the sustained period would be experienced as more unpleasant than the opening; and that somatic anxiety would
increase after viewing. We also tested whether the group-level affective time course reproduced across samples.
Further hypotheses examined whether everyday fear of heights was associated with a stronger response, whether
increases in somatic anxiety accompanied higher arousal and more unpleasant valence, and whether retrospective
anxiety ratings agreed with plateau arousal and somatic change.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Preregistration and Transparency}

The hypotheses and analysis plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; \url{https://osf.io/s2yvb}).
All confirmatory tests followed the registered plan, and exploratory analyses are labelled as such.

\subsection{Rating Platform and Data Integrity}

The platform is a jsPsych task \parencite{deleeuw2023jspsych} hosted on a JATOS server \parencite{lange2015jatos}
and runs in an ordinary desktop web browser. It is open source (MIT licence), and the version used here is archived
as \texttt{v1.0-heights-film} (see Data Availability). Participants rate on a small affect grid
\parencite{russell1989affectgrid} overlaid on the video (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a; Online Resource 1, Fig.~S2).
The mouse pointer is hidden and locked, so moving the mouse moves the dot without clicking. Horizontal position codes
valence (0~=~unpleasant, 100~=~pleasant) and vertical position arousal (0~=~low, 100~=~high). The dot position is
sampled at 20~Hz together with the video, frame and elapsed time, and every raw input event is stored. The film is
downloaded in full before playback, playback controls and seeking are disabled, leaving the tab or full screen pauses
the film, and data are saved every 30~s so that a session resumes after a page reload. Before the film, participants
practise the mouse control by tracking a moving target and by placing six everyday situations on the grid, with
feedback. The same session can present Likert-type scales, multiple-choice and checklist items and free-text entry.
Several features support data integrity: two instructed-response attention checks, blocked pasting and logged
keystrokes in text fields, counts of script-generated browser events, and quality-control code that summarises
coverage, interruptions and rating activity and flags unusual mouse movement and text entry for review. Participants
were excluded by the registered rules alone (Online Resource 1, Table~S1). Section S1 of Online Resource 1 describes
each feature.

\subsection{Participants}

Participants were recruited on Prolific \parencite{palan2018prolific}. Filters required fluent English, normal or
corrected-to-normal vision, an approval rate of at least 95\%, at least 20 previous submissions and a desktop
computer with a mouse or trackpad. Recruitment ran in cohorts with a 50/50 quota on Prolific's sex screener, and
participants were paid \pounds 4.80. The information sheet advised people with a strong fear of heights not to take
part. The pilot and the first cohort formed the exploratory sample, which was used to fix the segment edges, the
exclusion rules and the sample size. Later cohorts formed the confirmatory sample, on which all confirmatory tests
were run. Recruitment stopped at the end of the first cohort that brought the number of usable confirmatory
participants to at least 120, which gives 80\% power to detect correlations of about .25. The study involved watching
a film and answering questionnaires and was not a health research project under the Danish Act on Research Ethics
Review of Health Research Projects and Health Data Research Projects. For such studies there is no legal requirement
for ethical approval in Denmark, and Aarhus University does not require it. The Aarhus University Research Ethics
Committee provides a standard statement to this effect. All participants gave informed consent online before taking
part.

\subsection{Stimulus}

The stimulus was a 13 min 28 s first-person video of an unsecured climb to the roof of a skyscraper
\parencite{tikhomirov2017dubai}, presented without sound (1,280~$\times$~720 pixels, 25 frames per second).

\subsection{Procedure and Measures}

After consent and a device check, participants rated their everyday fear of heights (\enquote{In everyday life, how
afraid are you of heights?}, 1--7) and completed the practice. They then completed the somatic subscale of the STICSA
with state instructions (11 items, 1--4, total 11--44; \cite{ree2008distinguishing}), watched the film while rating
continuously, and completed the STICSA again immediately afterwards. Instructions asked participants how they felt at
that moment. Participants then rated how tense or anxious they had felt overall while watching (1--7) and answered
further questions about their experience and about data quality. They also wrote an account of what they saw and how
the film made them feel (at least 100 words), with a separate optional field for feedback on the study or technical
difficulties.

\subsection{Data Processing and Exclusions}

Ratings were resampled to 1~Hz of film time (808 values) by linear interpolation. The film was divided into four
preregistered segments, estimated in the exploratory sample by piecewise-linear segmentation of group-mean arousal:
baseline (0--262~s), rise (262--470~s), plateau (470--692~s) and return (692--808~s). Participants were excluded if
they failed both attention checks, produced script-generated input, did not type their written account (fewer than
0.2 keystrokes per character for accounts of at least 200 characters), gave erratic input (more than 10\% of mouse
movements larger than 250 pixels), had disrupted viewing (more than 30~s of buffering or 60~s of interruptions),
rated during less than 95\% of the film, or had seen the film before (Online Resource 1, Table~S1).

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

All tests were two-sided ($\alpha$~=~.05) with Holm correction within each hypothesis family, and a directional
hypothesis counted as supported only if the test was significant in the predicted direction. H1 compared STICSA
totals after and before the film (paired $t$ test; $d_z$ with exact 95\% CI; Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a check).
H2 tested four contrasts between participants' segment means with paired $t$ tests (arousal: rise $>$ baseline,
plateau $>$ baseline, return $<$ plateau; valence: plateau $<$ baseline). H3 correlated the confirmatory and
exploratory group-mean series (Pearson $r$; 95\% CI from 5,000 circular block bootstrap resamples with 30-s blocks)
and was supported if the lower bound exceeded .50. H4--H6 were Spearman correlations with bootstrap 95\% CIs (5,000
resamples): everyday fear of heights with plateau arousal, plateau valence and STICSA change (H4); STICSA change with
whole-film arousal and valence (H5); and post-film anxiety with STICSA change and plateau arousal (H6). All tests were
repeated in two registered sensitivity samples (stricter engagement criteria; participants rejected on Prolific
removed). Exploratory analyses examined the change in each STICSA item (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected
across items) and the effects of age and sex (mixed models of segment means). Analyses were run in Python
\val{python_version} with NumPy, SciPy, pandas and statsmodels. Large language model tools (ChatGPT and Codex, OpenAI;
Claude Code, Anthropic) assisted with writing and testing the analysis code and with preparing the manuscript. The
analysis code was checked with tests on small simulated datasets with known answers, and the author verified the
analyses, results and references and takes full responsibility for the work.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Online Implementation and Data Quality}

Of \val{pq:conf:completed} completed confirmatory sessions, \val{pq:conf:usable} (\val{pq:conf:pct_usable}\%) met
the registered criteria. The exclusions were for disrupted viewing (\val{n_excl_disrupted} sessions), an untyped
written account (\val{n_excl_freetext}) and erratic input (\val{n_excl_erratic}); no session was excluded for
script-generated input, low coverage, failing both attention checks or having seen the film. In the exploratory sample, \val{pq:expl:usable} of
\val{pq:expl:completed} completed sessions were usable. Buffering occurred in \val{pq:conf:n_any_buffering}
confirmatory sessions and interruptions in \val{pq:conf:n_any_interruption} (longest \val{pq:conf:buffering_max}~s
and \val{pq:conf:interrupted_max}~s), and ratings covered a median of \val{pq:conf:coverage_median}\% of film seconds
(minimum \val{pq:conf:coverage_min}\%). Of the completed sessions, \val{pq:conf:att_none} passed both attention
checks and \val{pq:conf:att_one} failed one, and \val{pq:conf:attentive_yes} participants reported watching with
full attention, \val{pq:conf:attentive_mostly} mostly and \val{pq:conf:attentive_no} not. Among retained
participants, ratings changed in a median of \val{pq:conf:bouts_median} bouts per minute (interquartile range
\val{pq:conf:bouts_q1}--\val{pq:conf:bouts_q3}), and the median longest stretch without input was
\val{pq:conf:gap_median}~s, which may reflect holding the dot still while feelings were unchanged. The
\val{pq:conf:n_sparse} participants who met the registered definition of sparse rating were removed in the stricter
sensitivity sample. Split-half reliability of the group-mean series was \val{rel:arousal} for arousal and
\val{rel:valence} for valence. Page reloads were not recorded in the exported data. The retained sample had a mean age of \val{age_mean} years ($SD$~=~\val{age_sd}; \val{n_female} female,
\val{n_male} male, \val{n_sex_missing} not available). Sample characteristics, descriptive statistics and all
confirmatory tests, including both sensitivity samples, are given in Online Resource 1 (Tables S2--S5).

\subsection{Somatic Anxiety and Affective Time Course}

\paragraph{Somatic anxiety (H1).} STICSA somatic scores rose by \val{H1:diff} points, from $M$~=~\val{sticsa_pre_mean}
($SD$~=~\val{sticsa_pre_sd}) before the film to $M$~=~\val{sticsa_post_mean} ($SD$~=~\val{sticsa_post_sd}) after it,
\val{H1:report} (Figure~\ref{fig:sticsa}a). Scores rose for \val{n_increase} participants and fell for
\val{n_decrease}, and the Wilcoxon test agreed (\val{H1:wilcoxon}). In the exploratory item analysis, all eleven
items increased ($p_\mathrm{Holm}$ \val{items:pholm_max}; Figure~\ref{fig:sticsa}b; Online Resource 1, Table~S6).
The largest change was in a fast heartbeat ($d_z$~=~\val{items:rank1:dz}) and the smallest in a dry throat, weak
muscles and stiff limbs ($d_z$~=~\val{items:rank11:dz}--\val{items:rank9:dz}).

\paragraph{Time course (H2, H3).} Arousal was higher than at baseline in both the rise segment, \val{H2a:report}, and
the plateau, \val{H2b:report}, and it was lower in the return segment than on the plateau, \val{H2c:report}
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b; Online Resource 1, Fig.~S3). Valence was lower on the plateau than at baseline,
\val{H2d:report} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}c). The confirmatory group-mean series closely followed the shape of the
exploratory series, for arousal \val{H3arousal:report} and for valence \val{H3valence:report}.

\paragraph{Individual differences (H4--H6).} Participants whose STICSA scores rose more gave higher whole-film arousal
ratings, \val{H5a:report}, and lower whole-film valence ratings, \val{H5b:report} (Figure~\ref{fig:sticsa}c, d).
Post-film anxiety correlated with STICSA change, \val{H6a:report}, and with plateau arousal, \val{H6b:report}.
Everyday fear of heights was associated with more negative plateau valence, \val{H4b:report}. Its registered
associations with plateau arousal, \val{H4a:report}, and with STICSA change, \val{H4c:report}, were not supported
(all individual-difference tests: Online Resource 1, Fig.~S4).

\paragraph{Somatic anxiety and ratings by segment (exploratory).} In a post hoc exploratory analysis of both samples
($n$~=~\val{xseg:n}), STICSA change was correlated with the mean ratings in each segment (Spearman $\rho$ with
bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals, uncorrected; Figure~\ref{fig:sticsasegments}). At baseline there was no clear
association (arousal \val{xseg:arousal:baseline}; valence \val{xseg:valence:baseline}). Valence was associated with
STICSA change in the rise (\val{xseg:valence:rise}) and plateau (\val{xseg:valence:plateau}), and arousal in the
plateau (\val{xseg:arousal:plateau}) but less clearly in the rise (\val{xseg:arousal:rise}). In the return segment
the associations were weaker (arousal \val{xseg:arousal:return}; valence \val{xseg:valence:return}).

\paragraph{Sensitivity and additional analyses.} All registered support decisions were unchanged in both sensitivity
samples ($n$~=~\val{n_stricter} and \val{n_without_rejected}; Online Resource 1, Table~S5). In the registered
descriptive analysis, segment edges re-estimated in the confirmatory sample fell at \val{edge1:est}, \val{edge2:est}
and \val{edge3:est}~s, against the registered \val{edge1:reg}, \val{edge2:reg} and \val{edge3:reg}~s. Exploratory
analyses described where on the affect grid the ratings fell in each segment (Online Resource 1, Fig.~S5) and found
no evidence that the time course differed by sex (arousal: \val{agesex:arousal:sex_x_segment}; valence:
\val{agesex:valence:sex_x_segment}) or by age, or that STICSA change was associated with sex or age (Online
Resource 1, Table~S7 and Figs.~S6--S7).

\section{Discussion}

A film of other people in danger elicited sustained unpleasant arousal in viewers who were themselves safe. The
affective response followed a closely reproducible course across independent samples, and viewers reported
substantially more bodily symptoms of anxiety after watching. These findings support the film as a strong and
consistent emotion-induction stimulus. The accompanying open platform provides a means of characterising how affect
unfolds during naturalistic stimulation.

The film's effectiveness is supported by convergence between continuous affect ratings, retrospective anxiety and
reported bodily symptoms. Somatic anxiety increased in nearly nine out of ten viewers, and all confirmatory
conclusions were unchanged under stricter screening. Established film-validation studies have characterised stimuli
according to the intensity and nature of the emotions they evoke \parencite{gross1995emotion,schaefer2010assessing}.
The present findings complement this approach by characterising the progression and duration of the response,
providing a sustained induction relevant to research using suspenseful films to study anxiety and brain--body
interactions \parencite{kirk2024brainheart}.

The film was selected specifically for naturalistic neuroimaging because it contains an extended, relatively
uneventful opening, rising tension, sustained danger and a quiet ending as the climbers leave the skyscraper.
Continuous ratings closely followed this intended progression. This temporal structure offers a useful basis for
studying dynamic functional connectivity, including comparisons across phases and changes within periods of
sustained arousal. The reproducible group trajectories also provide a reference for independently collected neural
and physiological recordings. Emo-FilM demonstrates the value of this approach by combining continuous emotion
annotations with physiological and fMRI data from a separate sample \parencite{morgenroth2025emofilm}. Such
reference curves can guide analyses while allowing for differences in individual experience.

The film also has practical advantages for neuroimaging. Its narrative is readily understood without sound, allowing
use in scanning protocols where audio delivery is constrained. Its visually engaging events and multiple people on
screen accord with calls to select naturalistic stimuli according to their content, presentation and experimental
purpose \parencite{grall2022leveraging}. Social content may be particularly useful: \textcite{finn2021movie} found
that clips with greater social content supported stronger prediction of cognitive traits from functional
connectivity. The first-person viewpoint may further promote immersion by presenting the height and movement from
the climber's perspective.

Larger increases in somatic anxiety accompanied higher arousal and more unpleasant valence, with exploratory
associations most evident during the rise and plateau. This links the film's affective progression to reported
bodily experience and provides a setting for studying how bodily sensations are interpreted within changing
emotional contexts \parencite{cristaldi2026beyond}. Given the modest correspondence between subjective experience
and physiology reported in previous film studies \parencite{mauss2005tie}, combining the task with physiological
recordings could examine how felt and measured bodily responses relate during rising, sustained and declining
arousal.

The platform supports this work within a browser-based workflow. Tools such as DARMA and PAGAN have already made
continuous annotation more accessible \parencite{girard2018darma,melhart2019pagan}. The present platform combines
simultaneous valence and arousal ratings within the video with participant training, video and displayed-frame
timestamps, interruption handling and input records. These features allow films to be characterised online before
laboratory use and support explicit quality-control decisions. Input logs also permit review of potentially assisted
or automated responding, although their detection accuracy has not been established. The task, quality-control code
and deployment scripts are openly available for adaptation to other stimuli.

Several questions remain for subsequent validation. Comparisons with other films could clarify the contribution of
height-related danger to the observed response. Continuous rating preserved subjective emotional engagement during
amusing and sad films but altered activity in regions associated with introspection
\parencite{hutcherson2005attention}, so transfer to passive viewing warrants testing. Somatic symptoms were assessed
only before and after the film, leaving their time course unresolved. Online recruitment and advice that people with
a strong fear of heights should not participate also limit generalisation to those with severe height-related fear.

The platform and reference data provide a reusable basis for studying sustained emotion. The film's reproducible
progression offers a common sequence through which individual affective and bodily experiences can be related to
physiological and neural responses.
